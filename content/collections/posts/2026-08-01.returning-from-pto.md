# Returning from PTO Without Losing a Day to Catching Up

You come back from a week off. You have 400 Slack messages, 12 open PRs, and a sprint that started without you. Most people spend the whole first day "catching up." Here's how to do it in an hour.

The key insight is that catching up is not the same as reading everything. Reading everything is how you lose the day. Catching up means getting to a state where you can make decisions again — and for that, you only need a fraction of what landed in your absence.

## The Reading Order That Actually Works

Don't open Slack first. You'll get pulled into threads from four days ago that have already resolved themselves.

Start with structure, not stream:

**1. Issue and PR board first.**

Filter your GitLab MR list to everything opened or updated while you were gone. You want two views:

- MRs waiting on your review: `https://gitlab.com/dashboard/merge_requests?reviewer_username=me&state=opened`
- MRs you authored that have activity: `https://gitlab.com/dashboard/merge_requests?author_username=me&state=opened`

Scan the titles and status. Merged = archived in your head. Open with comments = needs attention. Open with approvals and no action = probably blocked on you.

Do the same with your issue board. Use a sprint/iteration filter to see only the current cycle. Anything assigned to you that's sitting in "In Review" or "In Progress" is your first priority.

**2. Slack threads, not channels.**

Don't read channels top-to-bottom. Use `in:channel mentions:me` to surface anything that actually required your attention. Then go to your saved items — anything you saved before leaving that you haven't acted on yet.

After that, scan the threads in the channels you care about most using `has:reactions` to find the messages that generated real discussion. You don't need to read everything; you need to find the decisions.

## Saved for Later Is a Queue, Not an Archive

Before you go on PTO, anything you want to pick up when you return goes into Slack's "saved" list. This is a discipline. If you use it as a dumping ground for "interesting" things, it's useless. If you use it as a strict "I will act on this when I return" list, you walk back in with a ready-made action queue.

Same idea applies to browser tabs, Linear/Jira tickets, or email stars. One place. Strict criteria.

## Generating a Standup for a Day You Weren't Present

This one trips people up. You have a standup in an hour and you weren't there for any of the context.

Don't fake it. Don't write "Catching up on what I missed" — that tells your team nothing and signals you're still in limbo.

Write what's actually true:

```
Yesterday (returning from PTO):
- Reviewed sprint board — [feature X] merged while I was out, [feature Y] is waiting on my review
- Picked up [ticket Z] back from where I left it before OOO
- Flagged [PR #123] for follow-up — needs rebase before it can merge

Today:
- Review [PR #456] from @teammate — was waiting on me
- Finish [ticket Z] and move it to review
- Sync with @other if the [thing] decision got made while I was out

Blockers:
- Need a quick catch-up with @lead on the direction taken for [feature X] — I'll ping async
```

That's an honest standup. It shows you've triaged, you know what you own, and you have a plan. Nobody expects you to have been present — they expect you to be functional now.

I've started using a Cursor agent to generate this automatically. I run a "daily briefing" skill that pulls from Slack (mentions and saved items), GitLab (open MRs, CI status, recent merges), and Linear (sprint board state) — and compresses multiple days of context into a structured summary. On PTO return days I just run it for the date range I was out and it gives me a triage-sorted action list. The standup writes itself from that output.

## The Triage Pass

Once you have the full picture, go through everything with four buckets:

- **Act:** Needs something from you today. Review, reply, unblock.
- **Delegate:** Someone else should own this now, or already does. Confirm and move on.
- **Snooze:** Real, but not today. Set a reminder in Linear or Slack and close the tab.
- **Archive:** Resolved while you were gone, or no longer relevant. Mark read, close thread.

Most of what felt urgent in your absence falls into snooze or archive. The act bucket is usually smaller than you think.

## What to Communicate to the Team

On day one back, send one message per relevant channel — not a reply to every thread. Something like:

> Back from PTO. Triaged the sprint board and MR queue. Picking up [X] and [Y] today. If anything needs me urgently that I've missed, ping me directly.

This resets expectations, signals that you're functional, and preempts anyone pinging you about things you've already handled.

## The Full System, In Order

1. Filter GitLab MRs: authored and reviewer views, current sprint
2. Check issue board: what's assigned to you, what's stalled
3. Slack: `mentions:me` first, then saved items, then `has:reactions` scan
4. Run your briefing tool or do the triage pass manually (act / delegate / snooze / archive)
5. Write your standup from what you actually know, not what you wish you knew
6. Send one team message per channel to reset expectations

Total time if you're disciplined: 45 minutes to an hour. The rest of the day you're actually working.

The goal isn't to have read everything. It's to know what you need to do and to have a clean handshake with your team. Everything else was either handled without you or it can wait until tomorrow.
