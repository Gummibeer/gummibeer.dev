The framing I keep seeing is "AI writes my code." That's not what's happening in my workflow, and every time I try to describe it that way it sounds wrong. What I actually have is closer to an infinitely patient senior engineer who has read every doc, never gets tired, and can run five investigations in parallel — but has absolutely no political intuition and needs me to do all the actual deciding.

That reframe matters a lot. If you come in expecting an autocomplete that never hallucinates, you'll be disappointed fast. If you come in treating it like a force multiplier for the parts of engineering that are tedious or slow, it changes the shape of your work day.

Here's what that actually looks like for me.

## Before

I'm a senior engineer on a payments team. On any given morning I might need to:

- Triage three support threads that came in overnight
- Review four merge requests across two services
- Check whether a CI pipeline from yesterday recovered
- Figure out whether some Sentry noise is new or ongoing
- Surface the one thing from Slack that actually requires a decision vs. the ten things that are just FYI

Each of those is ten minutes if I'm disciplined and context-switch efficiently. Usually I'm not. I'd spend forty minutes reading Slack before writing a single line of code, still feel like I'd missed something, and by the time I was heads-down it was mid-morning.

The problem isn't volume. It's that the signals are scattered: one tool for issues, one for MRs, one for CI, one for support channels, one for standup. Stitching them into a coherent picture of "what matters today" is non-trivial synthesis work, and it happens before you've done anything.

## The Daily Briefing

The first thing I built was a skill — a structured prompt that functions like a runbook — that fires every morning before I look at anything else.

When I say "brief me," here's what it does simultaneously:

- Searches Slack for everything I was tagged in, saved for later, or mentioned in since yesterday
- Lists my open Linear issues sorted by priority and last activity
- Pulls all open MRs from GitLab where I'm a reviewer
- Reads the last few agent transcripts from previous sessions to pick up threads I left mid-thought
- Assembles all of that into a single markdown file and writes it to `TODAY-YYYY-MM-DD.md`

The output in chat is intentionally short — a handful of bullets under "Urgent / needs decision today" and a draft Geekbot standup. All the detail is in the file.

The daily file looks like this in practice:

```markdown
# Today — Thursday, 23 April 2026

_Last synced: ~09:15 CEST_

---

## 🔴 High Priority

- [ ] **DIR-4275** — Payout not adjusted after alteration with changed retained taxes
      (HOST-MZBLHN, $1,080.56 unpaid) — [Linear](…)
- [ ] **FFS-504** — Vrbo shadow tax calculation — MR !1291 Ready for Review (7 reviewers)
      — push toward merge — [Linear](…)

## 🟡 Reviews Needed

- [ ] [booking-service!3104](…) — Increase FS daily reservation cap to 50 (Aron, today)
- [ ] [financial-service!1306](…) — Implement early return in HideNonActionableTransactionsFilter

## ✅ Done Today

- [x] **FS reservation investigation** — reviewed 60 FS reservations (Apr 13–23),
      all good — signed off on scaling to 50 reservations ✓
- [x] [financial-service!1307](…) — add getRefundablePaymentSplits() — opened ✓
```

Every item has a direct link. Priorities are inferred from Linear's urgency field, Slack thread recency, and CI status. The agent doesn't assign meaning — it surfaces facts. I decide which one I open first.

The value isn't that it does triage for me. It's that I start the day with one place to look instead of five, and I don't have to reconstruct context from scratch. The file persists across the week, so carry-forwards from Monday show up in Thursday's list with a note that they're still unresolved.

## MR Reviews at Scale

On a team with multiple engineers all shipping across two or three services, I'm often listed as a reviewer on eight to twelve open MRs at any point. Some are genuinely urgent. Some have been sitting in "ready to deploy" status for a week waiting on a secondary approval. Most I need to read end-to-end before I can say anything useful.

What I used to do: open each MR, skim the diff, read the description, form a view, leave a comment. Twelve MRs is a half-day if they're substantial.

What I do now: fire a review agent on batches of MRs before I go to bed.

The prompt encodes what I actually care about in a review — not just "does this look right" but specific things: are there unhandled exception paths? does the migration have a corresponding down method? is there a test for the happy path and the failure path? does anything here change the shape of a webhook payload that downstream services depend on? Those questions live in a rule file that every review session inherits automatically.

By the time I look at the MR in the morning, I have a structured summary of concerns. I read it, decide if the concerns are real, add my own judgment on top, and either approve or leave a comment. My actual eyes-on-the-diff time drops from thirty minutes to ten — not because the agent reviewed it for me, but because it pre-digested the mechanical parts.

The things it misses consistently: context that lives in Slack rather than the MR description, whether this change is politically fraught between two engineers, and whether the approach is right even if the implementation is clean. Those require me.

## Parallel Investigations

This is the one that feels most like having a staff engineer in the room.

A couple of months ago we had a weekend incident. Payments were going wrong in a specific pattern — refund amounts were off in a way that didn't match any single code path. The kind of thing where you need to check three services simultaneously: what did the booking service record, what did the financial service do, what did Sentry capture?

Normally that's sequential. You check one thing, find a reference, chase it to the next, form a hypothesis, check the third. An hour passes.

What I did instead: spawned three parallel investigation agents. One told to look at the booking service side — recent events for the affected reservations, job audit trail. One told to look at the financial service side — payment splits, order state transitions. One told to pull and summarize the Sentry traces for the affected time window.

While they ran, I read through the MR that had deployed Friday. The agents came back with summaries. I synthesized. The root cause took about twenty minutes to identify instead of ninety.

The map-reduce framing is accurate: the agents do the map — gather, normalize, summarize — and I do the reduce — interpret, judge, decide. They're not producing conclusions. They're producing organized facts fast enough that I can hold the whole picture in my head at once.

## Where It Falls Apart

I've been genuinely surprised by how well this works for surface-area problems — the ones where the bottleneck is synthesizing a lot of inputs. But there are categories where it's close to useless without heavy supervision.

**Judgment calls about approach.** The agent will implement what you describe, and it will do it cleanly. But if you describe the wrong thing — a solution that makes sense locally but creates a worse problem upstream — it will implement that wrong thing very competently. The more senior the problem, the more important it is that the human has the right model before the agent touches anything. I've learned to slow down at the design step and only use the agent for implementation once I'm confident in the direction.

**Political and organizational context.** Why is that MR sitting in "ready to deploy" for a week? The agent doesn't know that the author and the reviewer have a disagreement about the underlying approach that hasn't been surfaced yet. That context lives in Slack DMs and in the informal understanding between engineers. The agent will cheerfully tell me the MR looks fine and suggest I approve it. That's technically correct and practically wrong.

**Knowing what not to do.** This is the hardest one. Experienced engineers spend a lot of energy not building things — noticing when a request is solving a symptom rather than a cause, when a feature will create more problems than it solves, when the right answer is "let's talk about whether we need this at all." The agent doesn't have that instinct. It will helpfully start scaffolding the thing you asked for. You have to be the one who says "wait."

**Novel debugging.** For anything where the clues are in the behavior pattern rather than the code, the agent is a useful search engine but a bad diagnostician. It can pull logs, search for similar issues in the codebase, summarize what Sentry recorded — but the moment of insight that connects them is almost always mine. I use it to gather, not to interpret.

## Skills and Rules as Encoded Convention

The thing that makes the workflow actually stick is that I've encoded my team's conventions into the agent's context, not just my own preferences.

Rules files handle the repeatable stuff: always import classes rather than referencing full namespaces inline, never remove existing comments without a documented reason, error messages must say what failed and what state the user's data is in. That last one matters on a payments team — a vague "payment failed, please try again" is not acceptable when what actually happened was "we captured funds but couldn't create the reservation." The rule encodes that standard; the agent applies it automatically in every review and every implementation.

Skills handle multi-step workflows: the daily briefing, MR review patterns, investigation runbooks. A skill is basically a structured prompt that I've refined over a few weeks of use — the kind of thing that started as a long instruction I typed every time and turned into a reusable piece of infrastructure when I noticed I was copy-pasting it.

The payoff is consistency. The agent applying a rule isn't creative — it's not going to catch everything — but it's consistent in a way that I'm not at 4pm on a Friday. It will notice that an exception was swallowed silently on the eighteenth MR review of the week even when I've started skimming. That matters.

## What Stays Human

Everything that requires judgment about impact, not just correctness.

Whether to merge something now or wait for a second opinion. Whether a bug is bad enough to warrant a hotfix or can ride the next deploy. Whether a support thread is a one-off or a pattern worth filing a ticket. Whether to push back on a feature request or implement it. How to communicate a delay to a stakeholder who's already frustrated.

None of that is in the agent's wheelhouse. It's not that the agent gives bad answers — sometimes it gives plausible-sounding ones — it's that those questions aren't answerable from code and tickets alone. They require the full context of the team, the product, the relationships, and the history. That's the job.

What the agent does is create headroom for that job. When I'm not spending two hours on synthesis and mechanical review, I have more actual thinking time. That's the value proposition — not "AI writes my code" but "AI handles enough of the low-cognitive-load surface area that I can spend my best hours on the parts that require a human."

## The Mental Model That Stuck

I landed on "staff engineer who's never been on this team before." They're extremely capable. They've read everything — every RFC, every doc, every language spec. They work fast and don't complain. But they don't know who on the team has strong opinions about a specific pattern, why a particular architectural decision was made three years ago, or which support thread actually requires escalation vs. which one is a user who'll figure it out.

That combination — high capability, no organizational memory — means you can delegate aggressively on the task, but you have to stay engaged on the judgment. You're not out of the loop. You're at a different point in the loop: earlier (design, framing, question quality) and later (synthesis, decision, communication) rather than in the middle (gathering, formatting, grepping).

Once that clicked, the tool made sense. I stopped trying to use it as a shortcut past thinking and started using it as a way to do more thinking on the parts that actually matter.
