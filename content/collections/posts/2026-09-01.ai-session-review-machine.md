Most tabletop role-playing groups talk about a session after it ends. The talk is usually brief.

"That fight was fun."

"The serious scene worked."

"We spent too long deciding what to do."

Then everyone goes home. By the next session, most of the useful detail is gone. A player may remember that a scene felt flat, but not why. The game master may know that the pace dropped, but not which part of the table caused it. The quiet player may have created three excellent moments that nobody noticed because another person spoke for most of the night.

I wanted a better review.

Not a build guide. Not a combat optimizer. Not a ranking of who "won" role-playing. I wanted something closer to an acting coach, an improvisation teacher, a scene director, and a group-dynamics analyst. It should look at how people create scenes, support each other, hold tension, make decisions, and give fictional events emotional weight.

The raw material already existed. We had session transcripts, summaries, and earlier feedback. Cursor could read those sources and write a structured analysis. Notion could store the result. MyArchivist could provide structured campaign and session context.

The first version sounded simple:

> Read the transcript, review every player and the game master, score them, and save the result.

That request hides nearly every hard part.

Who consented to this type of review? Which source wins when the transcript and summary disagree? What happens when speaker attribution is wrong? How do you stop every competent person from receiving an 8? How do you compare a quiet player with a loud one? How do you compare a player with the game master? How do you give direct feedback without turning the exercise into a public judgment system? How do you store the result without losing the evidence and uncertainty behind it?

The useful system was not the model. It was the contract around the model.

This post describes that contract. It is a rough account of the design, the rubric, the publishing workflow, the failures, and the limits. All examples below are synthetic. Player labels, character labels, scenes, quotes, awards, and scores were created for this article and do not describe a real table.

## The need was coaching, not evaluation

The word "score" can send this project in the wrong direction.

A number looks like a verdict. It looks more objective than prose. It invites comparison even when the evidence is incomplete. Once the number enters a database, a chart can make it look scientific.

That was not the goal.

The goal was to create a repeated coaching loop:

1. Observe what happened.
2. Identify what created or reduced energy.
3. Explain why it mattered.
4. Suggest one or two behaviors to test next time.
5. Review whether those behaviors changed.

The scores support that loop. They make trends visible. They force the reviewer to make a decision instead of hiding behind vague praise. They are not a measure of human value, friendship, intelligence, creativity, or commitment to the group.

This distinction has to appear in the prompt and in the way the result is presented.

The system reviews behavior and effect within one session. It does not review the person.

It also excludes most mechanical optimization. Character builds, spell choices, equipment purchases, damage output, and tactical efficiency are outside the scope unless they had a clear effect on role-playing, group dynamics, pacing, or narrative effect. A tactically poor decision can be excellent role-playing. A tactically perfect turn can drain a scene if it takes ten minutes of rules discussion.

The review therefore asks questions such as:

- Did this person make their character feel distinct?
- Did they create a scene or only react to one?
- Did they build on another person's contribution?
- Did a joke support the tone or destroy it?
- Did rules talk interrupt a dramatic beat?
- Did the game master give the players something they could act on?
- Did a quiet contribution change the direction or emotional truth of the scene?

That is a coaching frame. A leaderboard is not.

## Consent is not a footnote

Session transcripts can contain private conversation, personal disclosures, voices, names, and details that were never meant to leave the group. A performance review can also affect trust at the table. The technical ability to analyze a transcript does not create permission to do it.

I would not use this system without clear consent at two separate levels.

### Consent to record and process

Everyone must know:

- that the session is recorded or transcribed;
- which services process the audio or text;
- where the transcript is stored;
- who can access it;
- how long it is kept;
- whether it may be sent to an AI system;
- whether the AI provider may retain or train on the data;
- how a person can ask for deletion.

Consent must be specific. Consent to record for private notes is not automatically consent to run a performance review.

### Consent to receive coaching

People must also agree to the review itself.

Some players want direct notes after every session. Some want only private feedback. Some do not want numbers. Some want group-level observations but no individual review. Some enjoy role-playing without trying to improve it as a performance practice.

All of those positions are valid.

A safe setup should let each person choose:

- individual prose feedback: yes or no;
- individual scores: yes or no;
- trend tracking: yes or no;
- private delivery or group delivery;
- whether their data can be used in group summaries;
- whether past reviews can be compared with the current session.

The default should not be public publication. I prefer private delivery first. A person can then choose what, if anything, they want to share.

The game master also needs consent. "You run the game, so you must accept a detailed review" is not a healthy assumption.

### Consent must remain reversible

Consent is not a permanent checkbox.

A player should be able to skip one session, stop future reviews, remove old reviews, or receive prose without scores. The system should not punish that choice with missing-data labels that make the person look uncooperative.

The safest product rule is simple:

> No consent means no individual review.

The group-level review can still discuss pacing or table energy, but it must not reconstruct a declined person's individual score through indirect language.

### Never surprise the table with a ranking

The most harmful version of this idea is easy to imagine: the game master records everyone, runs an opaque prompt, posts a ranked scorecard in the group chat, and calls it objective.

Do not do that.

If the system changes the social contract of the table, discuss it before the session. Show the rubric. Explain the scale. Explain that transcript errors exist. Explain who can see the results. Let people opt out without pressure.

The system should create more psychological safety, not less.

## The source hierarchy is part of the product

A transcript is not a perfect record. A summary is not a transcript. Earlier feedback is not evidence for the current session. Character notes are not proof that a player expressed a trait at the table.

The prompt needs an explicit source hierarchy:

1. Current transcript
2. Current session summary
3. Previous session feedback
4. Optional character or campaign notes
5. Optional long-term trends

This order solves several common errors.

### The current transcript is the primary source

If the summary says the group had a serious argument but the transcript shows that the exchange was brief and playful, the transcript wins.

If a character note says Character A fears authority but the player never expresses that fear in the session, the review must not award depth for information that stayed outside play.

If earlier feedback says Player B often interrupts, the reviewer must not repeat that claim unless the current transcript supports it.

### The summary helps with structure, not proof

Session summaries are useful for orientation. They help the reviewer locate major scenes, understand broad chronology, and identify references that speech-to-text rendered badly.

They are also compressed interpretations. They tend to preserve plot and remove hesitation, overlap, silence, repeated jokes, rules talk, and failed scene attempts. Those removed details are often exactly what a coaching review needs.

The summary can tell the reviewer where to look. It should not replace looking.

### Previous feedback is development context

Previous reviews make trend analysis possible, but they create anchoring risk.

If the last review said that Player C stayed too mechanical, the model will look for mechanical behavior in the next transcript. Without strict rules, it may interpret neutral evidence as confirmation.

The prompt therefore asks six development questions for each person:

1. Which earlier criticism was relevant to the current session?
2. Was there visible work on it?
3. Which improvements appear in the current material?
4. Which problems repeat?
5. Which repeated problems now deserve clearer language?
6. Which earlier strengths were confirmed or extended?

Then it adds the important guard:

> Nobody receives a lower score because of old criticism unless the current transcript supports the criticism.

The same rule applies to praise. A person does not keep an old reputation score after a weak session.

### Long-term trends come last

Trends can show that a pattern repeats. They cannot prove why it repeats.

A drop in focus may reflect fatigue, technical trouble, a weak transcript, reduced spotlight, or a different session format. A trend should start a question, not end one.

## Transcript uncertainty must remain visible

Speech-to-text systems produce confident nonsense.

They merge speakers. They split one speaker into two identities. They confuse character names with ordinary words. They remove pauses. They treat laughter as speech. They miss quiet lines. They assign table talk to the wrong person. They often fail when several people speak at once.

A review system that hides these limits will eventually make a harsh claim about the wrong person.

The prompt should expect:

- incomplete sentences;
- missing sections;
- incorrect words;
- uncertain speaker attribution;
- mixed in-character and out-of-character speech;
- jokes without enough tone information;
- silence that may be missing audio rather than passivity;
- duplicate transcript segments;
- unmarked breaks;
- tool or connection trouble.

The reviewer must separate, as far as possible:

- in-character speech;
- out-of-character planning;
- meta discussion;
- jokes;
- interruptions;
- silence or low participation;
- hesitation;
- technical problems.

It should also state uncertainty at the level where it matters.

Weak:

> Player B reduced the scene's tension with a joke.

Better:

> The transcript assigns the joke to Player B, but two speakers overlap in this section. I am not using this moment as a basis for a strong individual criticism.

That sentence is less neat. It is also more honest.

### Confidence should affect claim strength

I use a simple internal evidence model:

- **High confidence:** clear speaker, clear scene context, and the same interpretation is supported by surrounding lines.
- **Medium confidence:** speaker is likely correct, but tone or context is incomplete.
- **Low confidence:** speaker is uncertain, lines overlap, or the transcript conflicts with another source.

High-confidence evidence can support a direct criticism and a score.

Medium-confidence evidence can support a tentative observation, preferably with another example.

Low-confidence evidence can be mentioned as uncertainty. It should not support a harsh score or a repeated-pattern claim.

The final review does not need a confidence number beside every sentence. It does need a short source-quality note and clear uncertainty language where attribution matters.

### Absence of evidence is not evidence of passivity

This is especially important for quiet players.

A transcript may not capture facial expressions, private text chat, map actions, camera behavior, hand signals, or a supportive reaction that never became speech. Low word count alone does not prove low contribution.

At the same time, the system should not inflate a quiet player's score because it feels unfair to give a low number. The correct answer can be:

> The available transcript shows little evidence for initiative. The low score reflects limited observed material, not a claim about the player's ability.

That is direct and bounded.

## Evidence rules: every criticism has to earn its place

Generic feedback is easy to generate and hard to use.

"Be more engaged."

"Role-play more."

"Improve pacing."

"Give other people room."

These statements are not coaching. They are labels.

The prompt requires every meaningful criticism to contain four parts:

1. What happened
2. Why it mattered
3. What would have worked better
4. One to three examples of a better line, behavior, or scene choice

For example, a synthetic coaching note might say:

> During the negotiation, Player A answered three questions as table logistics before Character A expressed a position. This made the character feel like a problem-solving interface rather than a person with values. A stronger choice would have been to state the character's fear first, then discuss the plan. For example: "I know the bridge is faster. I still will not cross it while the guard is watching."

That note is useful because the player can test it.

### Evidence should show effect, not only activity

"Player A spoke twelve times" is activity.

"Player A asked Character B a question that exposed a conflict and then let Character B answer without interruption" is effect.

The review should look for moments where a person:

- created energy;
- reduced energy;
- could have deepened the character or scene;
- influenced another player or the group;
- showed a pattern that may matter beyond one session.

These five required moments prevent the review from becoming a collection of favorite quotes.

### One moment can support several categories, but it should not be counted several times

A strong confession can show presence, depth, expression, narrative effect, and emotional effect. That does not mean the person automatically receives five very high scores.

The scorecard should ask a different question in each category:

- Was the person clear and present?
- Did the contribution reveal inner complexity?
- Did it create action?
- Was it expressed well?
- Did it improve the group?
- Did it change the story?
- Did it create emotion?

One excellent moment may answer several of these questions. The rest of the session still matters.

### Pattern claims need repeated evidence

One interruption is an event. Repeated interruption across several scenes is a pattern. Repeated interruption across several reviewed sessions may be a trend.

The language should match the evidence:

- "In this scene..." for one event.
- "Several times in this session..." for a session pattern.
- "This repeats an issue from the previous review..." only when both reviews contain current evidence.

The model should never upgrade a single awkward moment into a personality claim.

## The ten player review dimensions

The prose review for each player uses ten dimensions. These dimensions describe what to inspect. They are not all direct database columns.

### 1. Character presence

How clear, distinct, and alive did the character feel?

The reviewer looks for a recognizable voice, attitude, physical behavior, values, and decisions. A player can be active while the character remains invisible. "I move six squares and use the ability" is table activity. It is not character presence.

Presence does not require an accent or theatrical speech. A short, precise decision can make a character clear.

### 2. Role-playing depth

Did the player show values, fears, wants, contradictions, weaknesses, or internal conflict?

Depth is not the same as sadness. A comic character can have depth. A decisive character can reveal depth through what they refuse to do. The key is that the choice tells the table something specific about the fictional person.

### 3. Initiative and agency

Did the player start scenes, ask meaningful questions, make decisions, create risk, or push action forward?

Agency is not measured by speaking first. A quiet player who asks one decisive question may create more movement than a loud player who repeats the available options.

### 4. Dialogue quality

Did the player's words sound like the character? Were they specific, memorable, and suited to the scene?

The reviewer separates character speech from problem-solving language. It also checks timing. A good joke can strengthen a scene. The same joke can break a scene if it arrives before anyone can react to an emotional event.

### 5. Emotional or dramatic effect

Did the player create tension, warmth, humor, friction, vulnerability, conviction, or intensity?

The question is not whether the player performed a dramatic monologue. Small reactions can carry more weight than long speeches.

### 6. Group contribution

Did the player listen, build on other contributions, make room, support another person's scene, and help the table stay active?

The reviewer also checks for domination, interruption, derailment, withdrawal, and repeated rescue of other players' decisions.

### 7. Campaign effect

Did the player's choices matter to relationships, conflicts, tone, group identity, or future consequences?

This category protects the review from focusing only on performance style. A plain sentence that changes a relationship may matter more than a polished speech that changes nothing.

### 8. Missed opportunities

Where could the player have made more of the material already present?

This is not an invitation to invent a "correct" scene. It should identify an opening that existed in the transcript. The coaching note can then offer several possible responses rather than one canonical answer.

### 9. Coaching

What should the player try next time?

The advice must be small enough to use during play. "Develop the character more" is too broad. "Before your first tactical answer, state what Character A wants from the scene" is usable.

Each review should include at least three concrete examples of lines, behaviors, or scene choices.

### 10. Pattern recognition

What strength, weakness, or behavior pattern may continue across sessions?

The word "may" matters. One session is weak evidence for a long-term claim. Earlier reviews can increase confidence, but only if the current transcript shows the pattern again.

## The ten game master review dimensions

The game master needs a separate prose rubric because the role is different.

### 1. Scene setup

Were scenes opened with clear, playable information and useful hooks?

A description can be vivid but not playable. The players need pressure, desire, uncertainty, or a choice. The review asks whether the opening gave them something to do.

### 2. Non-player character portrayal

Did non-player characters differ in goal, attitude, speech, energy, and friction?

An information source is not automatically a character. The reviewer looks for wants and resistance, not only accents.

### 3. Spotlight management

Did every consenting player receive a fair chance to affect the session?

Equal minutes are not always the goal. Some scenes belong to one character. The useful question is whether the game master noticed who had not entered play and offered a meaningful opening rather than a token check-in.

### 4. Pacing

Where did the session drag, rush, lose pressure, or end a scene too early?

The review also looks at transition cost. Rules lookup, tool operation, repeated planning, and unfocused jokes can all drain momentum.

### 5. Improvisation and response

Did the game master turn player ideas into play?

The opposite failure is not "saying no." A clear no can protect the fiction. The failure is accepting an idea verbally but returning to prepared material without letting the idea change anything.

### 6. Stakes and tension

Were risk, consequence, desire, uncertainty, and conflict clear?

Mechanical danger is only one form of stakes. Social cost, lost trust, exposure, time pressure, and moral compromise can create stronger play.

### 7. Descriptive support

Did the game master provide enough sensory, emotional, and situational material for the players to respond?

Players cannot create detailed reactions to a scene that arrives as abstract data. The review asks whether descriptions provided usable texture without becoming a lecture.

### 8. Table leadership

Did the game master orient the group, resolve uncertainty, manage overlap, and keep play moving without controlling every choice?

This includes deciding when to stop rules debate, when to call for a decision, and when to let silence remain.

### 9. Role-playing enablement

Did the game master actively create room for character values, relationships, conflict, choice, and consequence?

Direct questions can help:

> "Character B, what about this offer makes you hesitate?"

That is stronger than:

> "Does anyone do anything?"

### 10. Coaching

What can the game master change in the next session?

As with player coaching, the answer must be concrete. "Improve pacing" becomes "After two repeated arguments, summarize the options and ask one named player for a decision."

## One shared scorecard for different roles

The prose rubrics are role-specific. The tracking system uses one shared set of ten score categories for every participant:

1. Presence
2. Depth
3. Initiative
4. Expression
5. Group contribution
6. Narrative effect
7. Emotional effect
8. Focus and discipline
9. Development
10. Overall value

This shared schema makes trend storage and charts practical. It does not mean that players and the game master perform the same work.

For a player, presence may mean character clarity and scene participation.

For the game master, presence may mean clear scene framing, distinct non-player characters, and confident table leadership.

For a player, initiative may mean starting a conversation or choosing a risky course.

For the game master, initiative may mean presenting active pressure, making a strong cut, or turning an unexpected idea into a scene.

The categories measure comparable effect within a role, not identical behavior across roles.

This point must be explicit. Without it, the scorecard produces absurd comparisons, such as treating a player's dialogue and the game master's pacing as if they were the same action.

## The 0-10 scale, with 5 as average

Most score systems become useless because 7 becomes bad, 8 becomes normal, and 10 becomes praise.

I wanted the midpoint to remain useful:

- 0 = absent or actively harmful
- 1 = extremely poor
- 2 = very weak
- 3 = weak
- 4 = below average
- 5 = functional or average
- 6 = solid
- 7 = strong
- 8 = very strong
- 9 = exceptional
- 10 = extremely rare and outstanding

Half points are allowed.

The most important line in the prompt is:

> Five is not bad.

A 5 means the person did the job in that category. A 6 means solid work. A 7 should require clear evidence of strength. An 8 should not appear because the reviewer wants to be kind.

Most competent scores should sit between 5 and 7.

### Anti-inflation rules

The prompt adds direct rules because polite language models tend to compress everyone toward high scores:

- Do not raise scores to protect feelings.
- Do not force scores to be equal.
- Use 0-3 when the observed effect was nearly absent, clearly weak, disruptive, or harmful.
- Do not give a high group-contribution score to a passive player without evidence.
- Do not punish a quiet player when their few contributions were strong.
- Reward quality over volume.
- Separate dominance from value.
- Treat 8, 9, and 10 as unusual.
- Give the best-supported score even when evidence is limited, then state the limit.

### Calibration needs anchors, not adjectives

"Strong" alone is vague. The prompt works better when each anchor has operational meaning.

A 7 in initiative should mean repeated, useful creation of play. One minor suggestion does not reach it.

A 3 in expression should mean that the person's role was often hard to hear because most contributions stayed functional, unclear, or out of character.

A 9 in emotional effect should require a moment or series of moments that changed the energy of the entire table.

The model still makes subjective judgments. The anchors make those judgments more consistent.

### Development is not a reward for having history

The development score is difficult when there is no earlier review.

If there is no reliable comparison, the output should say so. It should not invent progress. A neutral score may be used for database completeness, but the prose must mark it as low-confidence or not comparable.

When earlier feedback exists:

- visible improvement can raise development;
- repeated, previously named problems can lower development;
- neither may affect the score unless the current transcript shows evidence.

### Overall value is not a simple average

I do not treat overall value as the arithmetic mean of the other nine scores.

A single high-impact contribution can create major value even if expression was uneven. A person can also produce polished dialogue while repeatedly blocking decisions.

Overall value asks:

> What was this person's net contribution to the quality of this session, within their role?

The category scores explain that judgment. They do not calculate it.

## Awards create useful decisions and dangerous incentives

The review assigns up to three awards.

### Session MVP

Exactly one person receives the session MVP.

The person can be a player or the game master. The award means they created the highest total value for this session through role-playing, scene construction, emotional effect, leadership, humor, pacing, tension, momentum, or group support.

The prompt explicitly says:

- do not automatically choose the loudest player;
- do not automatically choose the game master;
- explain the net value;
- use evidence from more than raw speaking time.

For some groups, the term MVP may feel too competitive. Rename it or remove it. "Largest positive effect" communicates the same analysis with less sports language.

### Inspiration Moment

This award is optional.

It should only appear when a player's moment is an example for the whole table. A merely good or funny contribution is not enough.

Possible thresholds include:

- unusually honest emotional play;
- strong play despite social risk;
- an exceptional line that fits the character and scene;
- a meaningful escalation;
- a choice that shows character at a real cost;
- a moment that clearly raises the whole table's level.

If no moment reaches the threshold, the output must say:

> No Inspiration Moment in this session.

This explicit empty state matters. Otherwise, the model invents an award because the output structure contains an award field.

### Growth Target

Exactly one person receives the Growth Target.

This is not a loser award. It identifies the person with the largest practical improvement lever relative to their effect on the session.

A person can be useful and still receive it. In fact, the best growth target is often someone who already contributes but has one repeated behavior that limits their value.

The explanation must be direct, private by default, and paired with a concrete next action.

If the group cannot receive this award without shame or conflict, do not use it. A good analytical device is not always a good social device.

## The session itself also receives scores

Individual scores do not fully describe the session.

The system also tracks seven session-level categories:

- Role-playing quality
- Group chemistry
- Pacing
- Immersion
- Dramatic energy
- Narrative progress
- Overall session quality

These scores help separate individual performance from shared conditions.

A player can have a strong session inside a poorly paced game. The game master can create good scenes while technical problems damage immersion. A session can have little plot progress but high relationship development.

The prose must explain the numbers. A 6 in narrative progress should state whether progress came through plot, relationships, discovery, or a change in group identity.

Again, the chart is a memory aid. It is not the review.

## The machine-readable tracking block

Human-readable prose is useful once. Structured output is useful across sessions.

At the end of each review, the prompt emits a small plain-text block:

```text
SESSION_MVP: [Participant Label]
INSPIRATION_MOMENT: [Participant Label or NONE]
GROWTH_TARGET: [Participant Label]

PERSON_SCORES:
- [Participant Label]: Role=[Role] | Presence=X | Depth=X | Initiative=X | Expression=X | Group=X | Narrative=X | Emotion=X | Focus=X | Development=X | Overall=X

SESSION_SCORES:
- Roleplay=X | Chemistry=X | Pacing=X | Immersion=X | Energy=X | Progress=X | Overall=X
```

This format is intentionally boring.

It is easy to parse, easy to compare, and easy to inspect when a write fails. The keys remain stable even if the prose changes. `NONE` is explicit. Role labels are included. Decimal notation is normalized.

A JSON object could work too. I used a plain-text block because it is readable in the review itself and resilient when copied between tools. The important part is not the syntax. The important part is that the schema stays stable.

The parser should still validate:

- exactly one MVP;
- zero or one Inspiration Moment;
- exactly one Growth Target when that award is enabled;
- one score line per consenting participant;
- all ten participant categories;
- all seven session categories;
- values in the allowed range;
- allowed half-point increments;
- valid role labels;
- no unknown participant labels.

Never trust the model's machine-readable block because it looks structured. Validate it.

## The Notion data model

The review becomes more useful when each result is connected to the underlying session and participant.

I split the model into four concepts.

### Participants

One record per person who has consented to tracking.

Useful fields:

- display label;
- role or usual role;
- consent for prose review;
- consent for scores;
- consent for trend tracking;
- preferred delivery mode;
- active or inactive state.

The stored display label does not need to be a legal name. A table label or private alias may be safer.

### Sessions

One record per session.

Useful fields:

- session label;
- source status;
- transcript available;
- summary available;
- transcript quality note;
- review consent snapshot;
- link to the source record;
- relation to the session review.

Do not copy the entire transcript into every review record. Keep one controlled source and link to it.

### Session Reviews

One record per review run.

Useful properties:

- review title;
- reviewer system, such as Cursor;
- session relation;
- MVP relation;
- optional Inspiration Moment relation;
- Growth Target relation;
- seven session scores;
- executive summary;
- strengths;
- weaknesses;
- group-dynamics summary;
- source-quality note;
- prompt or rubric version;
- created time;
- relation to participant reviews.

The page body can hold the full narrative, priorities, awards, score explanations, and machine-readable block.

### Participant Reviews

One record per reviewed person per session.

Useful properties:

- participant relation;
- session relation;
- session-review relation;
- role;
- ten participant scores;
- what worked;
- what reduced effect;
- relevant moments;
- energy and effect pattern;
- coaching;
- next-session focus;
- evidence-confidence note;
- consent mode used for this review.

The page body can hold the longer development comparison and detailed examples.

### Why separate session and participant reviews?

One large page is easier to create. Separate participant records are easier to query.

With separate records, I can ask:

- How has Player A's focus score changed?
- Which coaching target repeated three times?
- Does the game master's pacing score move with the session pacing score?
- How often was an Inspiration Moment left empty?
- Are scores drifting upward over time?

The separation also supports privacy. A participant can receive only their own review while the game master keeps the group-level analysis private.

## The publishing workflow

The publishing process is more complex than "write one page."

My current workflow is:

1. Read the database schemas.
2. Resolve the session record.
3. Resolve each consenting participant record.
4. Confirm the consent mode for this review.
5. Analyze the sources.
6. Generate the full review and tracking block.
7. Validate award counts and scores.
8. Create the session review with core properties.
9. Create one participant review per consenting person.
10. Add long page content in separate updates when needed.
11. Link all participant reviews back to the session review.
12. Read the created records again and verify relations, scores, and text.
13. Report any missing or partial writes.

The schema read at step one is essential. Relation fields often point to a different database than their labels suggest. A participant relation may expect a participant page, not a review page. Guessing IDs from copied links is fragile.

The verification read is also essential. A successful tool response proves that the call returned success. It does not prove that every relation points to the intended record or that every participant was created.

### Keep previous reviews untouched

Each AI-generated review should be a new record with its own reviewer and rubric version. It should not overwrite a human review or a review from another system.

This preserves disagreement.

If Cursor and a human reviewer interpret a scene differently, that difference is valuable. Overwriting one with the other destroys the evidence needed to improve the rubric.

### Store source quality beside the result

A score without a source-quality note invites false precision.

Useful notes include:

- clear speaker attribution;
- partial speaker attribution;
- transcript missing a section;
- one source used only for chronology;
- summary conflicts with transcript;
- no reliable prior review;
- technical interruptions may affect observed focus.

The source note should be visible near the scorecard, not hidden in an internal log.

## Prompt design lessons

The prompt became long because the task has many failure modes. Length alone did not make it reliable. Specific contracts did.

### Start with the role and the negative scope

The role was not "tabletop expert." It was a performance coach for role-playing, scene effect, group dynamics, presence, timing, character consistency, and narrative value.

The negative scope was equally important:

- do not optimize builds;
- do not judge equipment purchases;
- do not reward damage output;
- do not turn tactical preference into acting criticism;
- do not invent facts;
- do not build harsh criticism on uncertain speaker attribution.

Models need to know what not to optimize.

### Separate observation from scoring

The prompt first asks for scene and behavior analysis. Scores come later.

If the model starts with numbers, it tends to write prose that justifies its first impression. If it first collects evidence, identifies energy patterns, and compares development, the scores have a better basis.

### Define the exact output shape

"Give each player feedback" produces different sections and levels of detail on every run.

The structured version requires:

- executive summary;
- group dynamics;
- one fixed review structure per player;
- one fixed game-master structure;
- priorities;
- awards;
- scorecards;
- session scores;
- trend block.

Each participant review has required sub-sections:

- what worked;
- where the person limited themselves;
- exact relevant moments;
- energy and effect pattern;
- development;
- coaching;
- next-session focus.

This is not formatting for its own sake. It prevents the model from giving detailed notes to the most visible person and two polite sentences to everyone else.

### Write the empty states

Reliable prompts say what happens when data is absent:

- no earlier feedback means no reliable development comparison;
- no exceptional player moment means no Inspiration Moment;
- uncertain attribution means no harsh individual claim;
- no consent means no individual review;
- weak evidence still requires a best-supported score only when scoring consent exists, plus a limitation note.

An empty state is part of the feature.

### Repeat critical calibration rules near the output

Long prompts lose instruction weight. The anti-inflation rules appear near the scoring section, not only at the top. The transcript-primary rule appears in the source section and again before the supplied material.

This is deliberate repetition. The most dangerous constraints should sit close to the step where they apply.

### Use examples for form, not hidden answers

Examples help the model produce useful coaching lines and stable machine output. They can also anchor content.

All examples should be generic or synthetic. Do not place a real person's previous criticism inside the prompt as a sample. The model will reuse it.

### Version the rubric

If the categories or calibration change, store a rubric version with every review.

Otherwise, a chart may show a score change that came from a prompt change rather than behavior change.

Versioning also lets you compare prompt experiments. Did stronger anti-inflation language reduce average scores? Did a better uncertainty rule lower unsupported criticism? Those are system-quality questions.

## Payload and tool failure lessons

The analysis was not the only hard part. Publishing exposed a different class of failure.

### One large write can fail for unclear reasons

A participant review contains relations, ten numeric properties, several long text properties, and a long page body. Sending everything in one tool call created parsing failures.

The error looked like a bad character or malformed JSON. Individual fields worked. The same fields failed when combined. The practical cause was less important than the operational lesson: a large payload creates a large failure surface.

The reliable pattern was:

1. Create the page with the stable properties.
2. Capture the returned page reference.
3. Add long content in a separate update.
4. Link the page to the parent review.
5. Fetch it again to verify.

This is slower. It is also easier to debug and resume.

### Pass structured objects as structured objects

Another failure came from treating a nested object as a hand-built JSON string. Quotes, line breaks, non-ASCII characters, and long text all become possible breakpoints.

When the tool accepts an object, pass an object. Do not manually serialize it unless the API requires a string.

This sounds obvious. It is easy to violate when copying a large generated payload.

### Do not debug writes inside the production database

Testing smaller payloads created temporary pages. That solved the parsing problem and created cleanup work.

A better design uses:

- a dedicated test database;
- a dry-run mode that validates property names and payload size;
- a deterministic draft label;
- an archive operation for failed writes;
- idempotency keys or a unique review key;
- a cleanup step that runs even after partial failure.

If the tool cannot delete pages, know that before the first experiment.

### Batch creation is not always safer

Creating every participant page in one call reduces tool calls but increases the cost of one malformed field. A partial batch response can also make retry behavior unclear.

One page per call is easier to resume. Small batches may be reasonable after the schema is stable. I would optimize for correctness first.

### A successful write needs a read-back

The final workflow queries the created review records and checks:

- expected count;
- correct role;
- correct session relation;
- correct parent review relation;
- all score fields present;
- long content present;
- no accidental duplicate drafts.

Tool success is not data integrity.

## A fully synthetic worked example

The following example is invented from scratch. It does not represent a real player, character, campaign, session, quote, or score.

### Synthetic source material

The table has three players and one game master.

- Player A plays Character A, a cautious scout.
- Player B plays Character B, an idealistic healer.
- Player C plays Character C, a proud mercenary.
- The game master runs the session.

The session contains three main scenes:

1. The group finds a cracked bell in an abandoned watch post.
2. A frightened courier asks the group to hide a sealed message.
3. Guards arrive and demand to search the room.

The synthetic transcript contains these relevant moments:

- Player A notices a second exit and immediately starts discussing movement distances.
- Player B asks the courier, in character, "Who gets hurt if we hand this over?"
- Player C makes two jokes while the courier describes being followed.
- The game master gives the courier a clear goal but little sensory detail.
- When the guards arrive, Player A says, "Character A blocks the door," but gives no reason.
- Player B refuses to surrender the message even after the guards threaten arrest.
- Player C stops joking, tells the guards, "Then arrest me first," and creates time for the others.
- The game master lets that choice change the scene and has the lead guard focus on Character C.
- A long rules discussion follows about whether the second exit can be reached in one turn.
- The session ends after the group escapes. Nobody asks Character B how the threat changed their beliefs.

Speaker attribution is clear except for one joke during overlap. The review ignores that joke for individual scoring.

No previous review exists.

### Synthetic analysis excerpt for Player A

#### What worked

Player A identified the second exit before the crisis. This gave the group a useful option and helped the final escape feel prepared rather than invented.

#### Where Player A reduced their effect

When the guards arrived, Player A declared that Character A blocked the door but did not express motive, fear, or intent. The action was useful, but the character remained unclear. The moment could have shown whether Character A was protecting the courier, controlling the group, or acting from fear.

#### Why it mattered

The door action was Player A's best chance to turn tactical awareness into character presence. Because the reason stayed unstated, the table received the plan but not the person.

#### A stronger alternative

Player A could keep the same action and add one short line:

- "Nobody opens that door until we agree on the lie."
- "I know this kind of search. They are not here for the courier."
- "Character A's hand is shaking, but they still take the door."

#### Energy pattern

- Energy created: finding the second exit before the crisis.
- Energy reduced: extending the movement-distance discussion after the group had already chosen to escape.
- Missed depth: no reason was given for blocking the door.
- Group effect: the exit discovery gave everyone a workable plan.
- Possible pattern: strong spatial problem-solving, weak translation of plans into character voice.

#### Next-session focus

- Add one motive sentence to the first tactical action in each major scene.
- Ask one other character what they are afraid will happen.
- Let one uncertain decision remain in character instead of moving immediately to rules talk.

### Synthetic analysis excerpt for Player B

#### What worked

Player B's question to the courier changed the scene from message logistics to moral stakes. The later refusal to surrender the message put Character B's values at a real cost.

#### Where Player B reduced their effect

After the threat of arrest, Player B repeated the moral argument three times without inviting another character to respond. The conviction was clear, but the repetition slowed the decision.

#### Stronger alternatives

- "I will not hand it over. Character C, will you stand with me?"
- "I have made my choice. I need yours now."
- Stop after the first refusal and let silence create pressure.

#### Energy pattern

- Energy created: asking who would be hurt.
- Energy reduced: repeating the same argument after the position was clear.
- Missed depth: no reaction after the escape showed whether the threat caused doubt.
- Group effect: gave Player C a moral position to support or oppose.
- Possible pattern: strong values, but a tendency to restate rather than pass the scene.

### Synthetic analysis excerpt for Player C

#### What worked

Player C changed behavior when the guards arrived. The line "Then arrest me first" converted earlier humor into a costly act of support. It gave the game master a clear target and gave the group time.

#### Where Player C reduced their effect

Two earlier jokes arrived while the courier described danger. The jokes did not build the courier's fear or reveal Character C's defense mechanism. They reduced tension.

One overlapping joke has uncertain attribution and is excluded from this criticism.

#### Stronger alternatives

- If humor is a defense mechanism, make it character-specific: "Character C smiles, but keeps watching the window."
- Wait for the courier's answer before adding humor.
- Use the joke to test danger rather than dismiss it: "If they are as harmless as you say, why are your boots full of blood?"

#### Energy pattern

- Energy created: volunteering for arrest.
- Energy reduced: early jokes during the courier's account.
- Missed depth: no transition showed why Character C became serious.
- Group effect: the sacrifice gave the others time and raised the scene's stakes.
- Possible pattern: humor under pressure can either support or flatten the scene, depending on timing.

### Synthetic game-master analysis excerpt

#### What worked

The game master gave the courier a clear need and allowed Player C's sacrifice to change guard behavior. This was good reactive play. The guards did not ignore the choice and continue with a fixed script.

#### Where the game master reduced the session

The watch post was described mainly through exits and distances. The players received tactical information but little atmosphere. The rules discussion about reaching the second exit continued after the dramatic choice was clear.

#### Stronger alternatives

- Add one sensory pressure detail: "Dust falls from the bell each time a guard hits the outer door."
- Ask intent before measuring movement: "Are you trying to escape unseen or reach the courier first?"
- Set a short ruling and continue: "You can reach it, but the lead guard will see you. Do you go?"
- After the escape, ask Player B: "What part of the threat still follows Character B?"

#### Energy pattern

- Energy created: turning Player C's sacrifice into a new focus for the guards.
- Energy reduced: allowing movement calculation to hold the scene.
- Missed depth: no aftermath question for Character B.
- Group effect: the clear courier goal gave all three players a shared decision.
- Possible pattern: strong response to player choices, but scene descriptions favor geometry over emotional and sensory material.

## Tradeoffs

The system creates value by forcing structure. That structure also creates risk.

### Consistency versus false precision

A stable rubric lets the group compare reviews over time. The same rubric can make a subjective interpretation look objective.

The answer is not to remove all numbers. It is to keep evidence, uncertainty, and rubric version beside them.

### Directness versus psychological safety

Soft feedback is often useless. Harsh feedback can damage trust and reduce experimentation.

The prompt should be direct about behavior and careful about identity. "This joke reduced tension in this scene" is useful. "You are an immersion-breaking player" is a harmful personality claim.

Private delivery and consent matter as much as wording.

### Comprehensive review versus review fatigue

A full review for every participant can become very long. People may stop reading.

The database can store the complete review while each person receives:

- one strength to keep;
- one growth target;
- one concrete experiment for next time;
- optional detail on request.

The long form is useful for the coach and the trend system. It does not have to become homework for the whole table.

### Stable schema versus evolving understanding

Ten categories are enough to support trends and few enough to remember. They are not a complete theory of role-playing.

If the group learns that "focus and discipline" unfairly penalizes a style of play, change it. Version the rubric and do not pretend that scores before and after the change are directly equal.

### Automation versus human ownership

Cursor can gather evidence and draft precise coaching. It does not know the full social context. It cannot see every facial reaction. It may misunderstand a running joke. It may miss that a player had a difficult day.

A human should review sensitive feedback before it is published. The group owns the meaning. The model does not.

### Trend value versus surveillance

Long-term tracking can reveal improvement. It can also make play feel monitored.

Track only what the group wants to use. Do not create hidden engagement metrics. Do not use review data to pressure attendance, compare friends, or settle arguments.

## When not to use this

Do not use the session review machine when:

- participants did not consent to recording, AI processing, and coaching;
- the group wants casual play without performance goals;
- trust at the table is already fragile;
- feedback is being used to win a personal conflict;
- the transcript has poor attribution and no second source;
- a player is in distress and the review would turn vulnerability into a score;
- the session included sensitive personal material that should not enter another system;
- the game master wants proof that one player is "the problem";
- scores will be published as a ranking;
- nobody has time or desire to act on the feedback;
- the model output will be posted without human review;
- the rubric does not fit the group's play style.

Also avoid it for one-shots unless the group explicitly wants a workshop-style debrief. Trend tracking has little value when no shared practice follows.

Sometimes the correct review is a human conversation with no transcript, no score, and no database.

## What I would change in the next version

The first useful version already had strong evidence and calibration rules. A production-quality version should add more system controls.

### Consent-aware generation

The participant list supplied to the model should already exclude people who declined individual analysis. The model should not receive data it does not need.

### Evidence references

Each major claim should carry a source marker that can resolve to a transcript segment without copying private text into the public review. This would make human verification faster.

### Confidence fields

Participant reviews should include an evidence-confidence field. Low-confidence reviews should be excluded from trend charts by default.

### Rubric validation

A validator should reject:

- scores outside the range;
- unsupported increments;
- missing categories;
- duplicate awards;
- an Inspiration Moment without a qualifying explanation;
- trend claims without a previous review;
- participant labels that are not in the consented input.

### Score drift monitoring

The system should periodically show the score distribution by rubric version. If the average slowly rises from 5.8 to 7.6 without a clear reason, the calibration has failed.

### Human edits as first-class data

If the game master changes a score or rewrites a claim, store the original draft and the final version. Those edits are excellent prompt-improvement data.

### Short delivery mode

The full review should remain available, but the default participant delivery could contain only:

- one observed strength;
- one observed limit;
- one next-session experiment;
- an option to open the detailed evidence.

## The real lesson is not about tabletop games

This project started as a way to review a role-playing session. The design lessons apply to many AI review systems.

If an AI evaluates human behavior, the difficult work is not asking for insight. The difficult work is defining:

- consent;
- source authority;
- uncertainty;
- evidence thresholds;
- calibration;
- empty states;
- private delivery;
- structured storage;
- validation;
- human ownership.

Without those parts, the output may still sound intelligent. It may even contain useful observations. It will not be a reliable system.

The session review machine became useful when it stopped trying to be an all-knowing critic and started acting like a bounded coach.

It reads the transcript as the primary source. It states when attribution is weak. It rewards subtle play, not volume. It uses 5 as average. It lets exceptional scores remain exceptional. It can leave an award empty. It turns criticism into a concrete next action. It stores the result in a form that supports trends without pretending that the trend is truth.

Most importantly, it runs only for people who asked for it.

That is the difference between an AI scorecard and a coaching practice.
