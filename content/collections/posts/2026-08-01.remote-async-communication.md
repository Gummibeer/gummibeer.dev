Remote work filters for people who can write. Not prose — context. The ability to send a Slack message that doesn't require a follow-up question is the highest-leverage communication skill in an async team, and most engineers underestimate it badly.

I wrote about working at Hospitable — the culture, the structure, the way the team operates. This post is different. It's about the specific writing habits I had to build, some of which took embarrassingly long to internalize, because they're not obvious until you've been burned by their absence a few times.

## The "Context-Complete" Message

The test I use for everything I write is: would this make sense to someone who has no context at all?

Not someone who was in last week's standup. Not someone who saw the Slack thread two days ago. Someone who just joined the team this morning and only has access to what I wrote.

A context-complete message has:

- What situation prompted this
- What the current state is
- What action is needed and by whom
- What the deadline or urgency is

Most messages fail at step one. They assume the reader knows why this message exists. In an async team with people across four time zones, that assumption costs hours.

## Incident Updates

This is where vague writing has the most damage potential. When something is broken and the team needs to coordinate, the update has to carry the situation to someone who just woke up.

Here's what a bad one looks like:

> Still investigating the booking issue. Will update when I know more.

That is a sentence that contains no information. What booking issue? What's the impact? What have you ruled out? What's the next step? When will you know more?

Here's what a good one looks like:

> **State:** Identifying root cause. Booking creation is failing for all properties using the Stripe payment method. Bank transfer is unaffected.
>
> **Impact:** ~40 new bookings failed in the last hour. Hosts have not been charged. Guests have not received confirmation emails.
>
> **Next action:** Reviewing Stripe webhook logs. Suspecting a signature mismatch after the key rotation this morning.
>
> **ETA:** Update in 30 minutes or sooner if I find it.

State, impact, next action, ETA — in that order. Every time. This format tells the on-call engineer exactly how urgent the problem is, whether money is involved, and what's already been done. They can take over without pinging you.

This format feels like overkill for small incidents. Write it anyway. The habit matters more than any individual incident.

## Bug Reports

A bug report is not a description of what you saw. It's a reproducible path to the problem. These are different things, and conflating them is one of the most common ways engineers waste each other's time.

A bad bug report:

> The calendar isn't showing the right availability. Checked it for one of our properties and the blocked dates aren't appearing.

A good one:

> **Reproduction steps:**
>
> 1. Go to a property with at least one manually blocked date in the next 30 days (e.g. Property ID 12345)
> 2. Open the availability calendar
> 3. Observe: blocked dates show as available
>
> **Expected:** Blocked dates appear greyed out and unavailable
>
> **Actual:** Blocked dates show as available (same visual state as open dates)
>
> **Environment:** Staging. Reproduced in Chrome and Firefox. Not reproduced in production — the difference may be the calendar data sync job that ran on staging at 09:14 UTC.
>
> **Hypothesis:** The sync job may be overwriting manual blocks. Haven't confirmed this yet.

Notice the last part. The diagnosis — your hypothesis about the cause — is separate from the reproduction steps. One is fact, the other is inference. Mixing them leads to someone "fixing" the diagnosis instead of investigating the actual problem.

## PR Review Comments

Code review is where async teams sink the most time to misunderstandings. You leave a comment, the author pushes a change, you ping back — this loop can take two days across time zones for something that would take two minutes in a room.

The fix is labeling every review comment with its weight:

- **blocking:** Must be resolved before merge. This affects correctness, security, or will cause a production issue.
- **suggestion:** Strong preference, but I'll merge without it. Take it or leave it and explain why.
- **nitpick:** Minor style or readability preference. Totally optional. Feel free to ignore.

Without labels, the author doesn't know whether your comment about variable naming is in the same category as your comment about a missing transaction boundary. They have to guess, and often they guess wrong — either blocking merge on something trivial, or skipping something critical.

With labels, the author can process the review in one pass: fix the blocking comments, respond to suggestions with a decision, acknowledge or ignore nitpicks.

I also add a summary comment at the top for anything that needs a discussion before the author even starts making changes:

> **Needs discussion before changes:** The approach here creates two database writes that could get out of sync if the second one fails. Let's decide whether to wrap this in a transaction or add a compensation job before you invest more in the implementation.

That saves everyone the frustration of reviewing a large diff twice.

## Standup Messages

Most async standups are noise. People write what they worked on in vague enough terms that no one can act on any of it.

The format matters less than the content. What I try to include every time:

- What shipped or moved forward yesterday (specific, linkable if possible)
- What is actually blocking me right now (specific enough that someone can unblock me without asking a follow-up)
- What I'm working on today (with any expected handoffs flagged)

A bad standup:

> Yesterday: worked on the booking alteration flow. Today: continuing that plus some review work. No blockers.

A good one:

> Yesterday: Shipped the alteration fee recalculation — MR 4821, deployed to production at 14:00 UTC.
>
> Today: Starting on the cancellation policy edge cases from MRS-412. Will need input from the product side on the "grace period" definition before I can finish the branching logic — tagging @sarah in the ticket.
>
> Blocking: Nothing today, but MRS-430 (the Stripe webhook retry logic) is waiting on a review from the payments team and has been in queue for 3 days.

The last point is important. Naming something that's stuck — even if it's not blocking you today — creates visibility. In an async team, things die quietly in review queues. Standup is a forcing function to surface them.

## Writing for Escalation

There's a specific type of communication that took me the longest to get right: escalating an urgent issue to a third-party vendor or to leadership, under time pressure, when you're frustrated.

The raw version of these messages always comes out wrong — too emotional, too vague, or too technical for the audience.

What works is treating it like an incident update with a stakeholder summary at the top:

> **Summary for stakeholders:** Guests at 34 properties are receiving incorrect check-in instructions due to an issue in [Vendor]'s API. This has been ongoing for 6 hours. We need a resolution or workaround within 2 hours to avoid a wave of guest complaints.
>
> **Technical detail for vendor support:** [Concrete reproduction, API endpoint, request/response examples, timestamp of first occurrence, ticket numbers from previous related reports]
>
> **What we need:** Either a confirmed fix timeline, or a workaround we can implement on our side.

This format works because the stakeholder reads the first paragraph and knows what they're dealing with, while the vendor gets the technical detail they need to actually diagnose the problem. No one has to ask a clarifying question.

## The Uncomfortable Truth

Most of these habits feel slow when you're first building them. It takes longer to write a good incident update than a bad one. It takes longer to format a bug report than to paste a screenshot. It takes longer to label every review comment than to just write comments.

The time is not actually saved by writing fast. It's saved by the person on the other end not having to ask a follow-up question. In an async team, that question costs four hours minimum — someone has to see it, understand it, send it, wait for a response, re-read the answer, and get back to work. Multiply that by the number of ambiguous messages sent per day across a team.

Writing clearly is the highest-leverage thing you can do as a remote engineer. The code matters. The architecture matters. But neither of them travels across time zones as well as a message that makes sense to someone who has no context at all.
