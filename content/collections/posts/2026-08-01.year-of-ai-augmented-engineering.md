A year ago, when someone asked how I used AI tooling, my honest answer was: code completion and rubber-ducking. The model would suggest the next three lines, or I'd describe a problem out loud and writing it down would unstick me. Useful, but not transformative. Roughly equivalent to a smarter autocomplete.

Today that answer is completely different, and the gap is harder to explain than I expected. It's not that the tools got dramatically better in every dimension — though they did. It's that the mental model shifted. I stopped thinking about what the agent could write and started thinking about what it could do. That's a different kind of tool.

This is what that shift looked like from the inside, and what actually changed in my daily work — including the things that didn't improve, and the things that genuinely got worse.

## The Autocomplete Era Was Already Over

The framing of AI as "code autocomplete that doesn't embarrass you" is a year behind. It was the right mental model in 2023. By early 2025 it was already a liability, because if you're using an agent the way you'd use autocomplete — one suggestion at a time, waiting to see what it produces, accepting or rejecting — you're leaving most of the value on the table.

The shift that changed my workflow wasn't a better model. It was the move to agents: persistent sessions with memory of the task, access to tools (not just the ability to generate tokens), and the ability to spawn parallel workstreams and synthesize the results. Once you understand what an agent actually is — a reasoning system that can take actions in your environment, not a fancy autocomplete — the use cases become obvious. And they're mostly not "write code for me."

In the transcripts from my own sessions over the past year, the most common tasks are: daily briefing, investigation, review, skill authoring, and vendor escalation. Code generation is on the list, but it's not the top of the list.

## What Got Faster

### Triage and morning context

I wrote about the daily briefing pattern in [Using Cursor AI as a Staff Engineer](/blog/14-using-cursor-ai-as-staff-engineer), but the part I understated there is the cumulative effect of it running every day for months.

What changes is not just "I get a briefing." What changes is that I've stopped carrying context overnight. The briefing rebuilds it. The living daily file — `TODAY-YYYY-MM-DD.md` — accumulates through the day: MRs reviewed, issues resolved, decisions made. By the end of the week it's a compact record of the last five days with links to everything. The cognitive load of "where was I?" when I sit down in the morning dropped to near zero.

The first week I did this it felt like a nice convenience. Three months in it felt like infrastructure. I notice on the days the skill breaks — wrong date, GitLab CLI not authenticated, Slack search timing out — because I start the morning context-blind and it costs me a real hour.

### Cross-service investigation

This is the one that surprised me most. On a team with a booking service, a financial service, a guest-vetting service, and a handful of workers, debugging a cross-service failure used to mean sequential spelunking. You'd check one service, form a hypothesis, check the next service, update the hypothesis, check the third service, usually find out your first hypothesis was wrong, backtrack.

What I do now: spawn three parallel investigation agents, one per service, with specific scopes. While they run I read the MR that went out before the failure. When the agents return I'm synthesizing organized facts rather than chasing clues. The 90-minute incident becomes 20 minutes. The insight is still mine — the agents gather, I reduce — but the gather step used to be the bottleneck and it isn't anymore.

The investigation skill I wrote encodes exactly this pattern: spawn scoped subagents, gather structured output, synthesize at the top. It has explicit instructions on what to do when evidence is ambiguous ("document what is known, what is unknown, and the best hypothesis with a confidence level") because the failure mode before I added those instructions was false confidence. The agent would find one signal, decide it was the root cause, and write a summary that sounded definitive. Those summaries were sometimes right. They were right with a confidence that the evidence didn't justify.

### Review at scale

On any given week I'm a reviewer on somewhere between six and fifteen open MRs across a couple of repos. That range exists because some weeks I'm heads-down on a feature and reviews pile up, then I work through the backlog. The pile-up problem was real: by the time I got to MR twelve, I was skimming in a way I didn't want to be skimming.

Running a review agent on a batch of MRs before I sleep means I start the morning with structured summaries. My attention is focused on the concerns the agent flagged, not on mechanically reading the diff. I still read the diff — the agent does not get to approve things for me — but my mental energy is front-loaded on the substance rather than the surface.

The things the agent gets right consistently: unhandled exception paths, missing tests for a branch it can see in the code, migration files with no down method, changes to a response shape that something downstream probably depends on. The things it gets wrong consistently: whether the approach is actually right, whether the MR should be merged at all right now, and anything that requires knowing what's happened in Slack over the last two weeks. Those require me.

### Vendor escalations

I didn't expect this to be on the list, but it is. When you're dealing with a vendor who's broken something in production — wrong session data being returned, a third-party identity verification service misbehaving — the first barrier to an effective escalation is the cost of drafting it. You need to be precise and firm without being hostile. You need to enumerate the evidence. You need to escalate without burning the relationship.

Drafting that document used to take an hour. Now it takes ten minutes: I describe what happened and paste in the evidence, the agent structures it with a summary, a timeline, observed behavior versus expected behavior, and a list of what I need from them by when. I edit, but I'm editing rather than drafting. The delta in quality from what I would have written in an hour to what we produce together in twenty minutes is small. The delta in my mental state is not small — escalation drafting is one of the more draining tasks in the job, and removing the drafting friction lowers the bar to escalating when you should.

## What Stayed the Same

Architecture decisions. Still entirely mine, still require the full context of where we've been and where we're going, still require judgment about tradeoffs that the agent doesn't have the history to evaluate. The agent is useful here as a sounding board — describe the approach, ask for second-order consequences I might have missed — but I've learned to be skeptical of its confident assessments of architectural choices. It will evaluate the idea in isolation, without knowing that a "similar" pattern failed for us two years ago for reasons that aren't obvious from the codebase.

Ownership and accountability. When something goes wrong in production, the person on the hook is the engineer, not the agent. This is obvious but worth saying: using agents for review and investigation doesn't distribute the responsibility. It changes the inputs to your judgment, not who exercises judgment. If the agent missed something in its review and I approved without catching it, that's on me.

Understanding what the customer actually needs. I work on a team where a support thread might look like a bug but is actually a workflow misunderstanding, or might look like a user error but is actually a product gap. Reading between the lines of support tickets is a skill that has not become faster or easier. The agent is useful for pulling together what's in the ticket and what the data shows, but the interpretation — is this a one-off or a pattern, does this person need a fix or an explanation, is this actually our fault — is still something I have to do.

## What Got Worse

I want to be honest about this section because most year-in-review posts about tooling skip it.

### Context drift

Sessions have limits. The agent's working memory across a long conversation degrades. The daily briefing skill works well when it runs at the start of a fresh session. The sixth chat in a complex debugging session — where I've pasted error messages, snippets, decisions, and intermediate findings — produces increasingly confused output. I've learned to start new sessions more aggressively, with a clear context handoff, rather than continuing in a context that's degrading. The right workflow requires discipline about session hygiene that wasn't necessary before.

### Dependency on tool uptime

When Slack search times out, I lose part of my morning briefing. When GitLab's API rate limits, the MR summary is incomplete. When the agent is slow, the parallel investigation that should take twenty minutes takes fifty. The workflow is now more capable than it was a year ago, and also more brittle in a new way — the failure modes are different rather than gone. I've built graceful degradation into the skills (note what's missing, don't hallucinate replacements), but the dependency surface is wider.

### The skill maintenance tax

I have nine skills and a handful of rule files. They work well when they're current. When something changes — a new Slack channel matters, a Linear project is renamed, a GitLab group reorganizes — the skills silently become slightly wrong. They still run. The output just degrades slowly until I notice I'm getting stale or incomplete context and trace it back to a skill that hasn't been updated. This is maintenance work, and it's invisible until it bites you. I've started treating skill files more like code than like documentation: they need to be kept in sync, they need to be tested adversarially, and they accumulate drift the same way any other configuration does.

### Shallow skill maintenance as a risk

There's a subtler version of the above that I'm still working out. Some things I used to do manually — certain kinds of log analysis, certain grep patterns I'd memorized, certain sequences I could run in my sleep — I now delegate. The risk is that the skill atrophies. I haven't had to do a particular kind of investigation manually in months. Whether I could still do it efficiently without the agent is a question I can't answer yet. I try to stay conscious of this by reading the agent's output rather than just accepting it, and by running the investigation myself periodically when the stakes are low. It's not a solved problem.

## The Staff Engineer Pattern vs. the 10x Developer Myth

The "10x developer" frame — AI as force multiplier for individual output — is the wrong frame, and I think it leads people to use the tools badly.

The pattern that's actually useful is what I think of as the staff engineer frame: you're operating at a higher level of abstraction, spending your time on design, synthesis, and judgment rather than execution and gathering. You're not doing ten times as much work. You're doing different work, and the shape of your day reflects what a more senior version of your role looks like.

That's a meaningful distinction. If you use AI to produce more code faster, you've increased your output — and also increased the amount of output that needs to be reviewed, maintained, and understood by your team. If you use it to improve the quality of your thinking and the speed of your information synthesis, the leverage is cleaner.

The limit of the 10x frame is also that it focuses on individual throughput. What I've noticed is that some of the highest-leverage things the agent does aren't about my output at all. A well-structured escalation that moves a vendor off a blocker. A thorough MR review that unblocks a teammate. A morning briefing that means I don't drop a ball someone else was counting on me to catch. Those aren't productivity metrics. They're reliability and coordination metrics, and they matter differently.

## What I'd Tell Someone Starting This Now

**Start with the daily briefing, not code generation.** The code generation use case is obvious and mediocre. The triage and synthesis use cases are non-obvious and excellent. The first question is not "what can the agent write?" but "what do I spend time gathering and organizing that I could instead have assembled for me?"

**Write your first skill for a workflow you've already done manually a hundred times.** The skill will be better if you understand the edge cases from personal experience. Start with the thing you do every day and hate doing.

**Invest in the rules files early.** The rules files are your team's conventions encoded as context. They make every session better without any additional prompting. The error message standard my team runs — error messages must say what failed and what state the user's data is in — lives in a rules file and applies automatically to every review and every implementation. That took me an hour to write once and has paid back in every session since.

**Be skeptical of confident summaries.** The output sounds confident whether or not the evidence supports it. Train yourself to check the underlying sources when the conclusion matters. The agent is useful because it organizes facts quickly, not because its interpretation of those facts is reliable.

**Plan for the day the tooling is down.** Not in a paranoid way, but know what your workflow looks like when Slack search is down and GitLab is rate-limiting and the session context is full. The answer should be "slower, but functional" rather than "I don't know where to start." Don't let the workflow become a hard dependency.

## What Didn't Change

The job. The work of being a senior engineer — understanding what the system should do, deciding how it should do it, communicating across a team, being accountable for what ships — is the same work it was a year ago. The inputs changed. Some of the tedious parts got faster. But the core of it is judgment about systems and people, and that's still entirely a human skill.

I've been working in software long enough to recognize the pattern where a new tool gets over-credited in the first year and under-credited in the fifth. The over-crediting usually happens because early adopters are using it for the easy wins — the things it's obviously good at — and those feel dramatic compared to before. The under-crediting happens later because the tool became infrastructure and infrastructure is invisible.

I'm somewhere in the middle of that arc. The agent is infrastructure for me now in a way it wasn't a year ago. I notice it more when it breaks than when it works. That's probably the sign that it found its actual place in the workflow.

The mental model I've landed on: the agent is a capable colleague who's read everything and remembers nothing about this team. Delegate the gathering. Delegate the formatting. Delegate the first-pass review. Keep the judgment. Keep the context. Keep the accountability. Know what you'd do if the colleague didn't show up.

That's roughly where I am after a year. The workload didn't shrink. It changed shape.
