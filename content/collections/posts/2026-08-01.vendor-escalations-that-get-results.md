You've filed three support tickets. You've been passed between agents. The product team says it's "under consideration." Your users are affected. Here's how to write the message that moves things.

## First: Know Which Mode You're In

Not all vendor failures are the same problem, and conflating them in your message is the fastest way to get a generic non-response. There are three distinct escalation modes:

**Bug report.** The product does something it isn't supposed to do. There's a spec violation, a regression, a broken API contract. This has a clear correct state and a clear wrong state.

**Product gap.** The product works as designed, but the design doesn't meet your use case. Your feedback is asking for a change to what they built, not a fix to what broke.

**Business risk.** The current behavior — whether a bug or a gap — is causing measurable harm to your users or your business, and continuing to wait is not a neutral choice.

The first two get routed to different parts of the vendor's org. The third changes the conversation entirely. Most escalations fail because they're framed as bug reports when they're actually business risk disclosures.

## Quantify Before You Write

Before drafting anything, collect:

- How many users are affected, and how often
- What the workaround costs (engineering time, support volume, manual labor)
- What happens when there is no workaround — failed transactions, churned accounts, SLA breaches
- How long this has been open

You don't need to be precise to the decimal point. You need to be credible. "This affects roughly 12% of our users attempting checkout on mobile" is more useful than "many users are having trouble." It tells the vendor's PM something they can put in a prioritization document.

If you can attach revenue to it, do. "We estimate this is blocking approximately X USD/month in conversion" is a sentence that gets forwarded to someone with authority.

## The Minimum Reproducible Evidence Package

Support agents triage by effort. If your ticket requires work to understand, it waits behind tickets that don't. Structure your evidence to eliminate that friction:

1. **What you expected to happen** — reference their own documentation where possible
2. **What actually happened** — exact error messages, API response bodies, not paraphrases
3. **Steps to reproduce** — environment, configuration, request/response pairs
4. **Workaround status** — whether you have one, what it costs, why it's not viable long-term
5. **Impact summary** — users, frequency, business effect

Put this in a single message, not spread across a thread. Paste the error verbatim. Attach the HAR file, the screenshot, the log extract. The goal is to make it effortless for someone who has never heard of your use case to reproduce the problem in under ten minutes.

## The Escalation Path

Support can triage and fix bugs. They can't change product direction or prioritize your request above others. If you've hit a wall:

**Customer Success Manager (CSM):** Your first non-support escalation. Frame this as a shared problem — you're not trying to complain, you're alerting them to something that's damaging your ability to retain customers or grow on their platform. CSMs have internal channels support doesn't. They also have incentives aligned with your retention.

**Account Executive (AE):** When the issue is commercial. Revenue risk, contract renewal implications, competitive pressure. AEs respond to business-level signals because those signals affect their numbers. Be specific about the timeline — "our contract renews in Q3 and this is a factor in that conversation" is a sentence that creates urgency.

The mistake most engineers make is treating escalation as an accusation. It isn't. It's giving the vendor information they need to make a better decision about their roadmap. Write it that way.

## The "We're Evaluating Alternatives" Message

Sometimes you need to say this. Done wrong, it reads as a threat and puts the vendor on the defensive. Done right, it's a factual statement about your situation that happens to create urgency.

Here's a message that works:

> We've been tracking this issue for [timeframe] and the current workaround is no longer sustainable at our scale. We've started an internal evaluation of alternative providers to understand what a migration would involve. We'd like to resolve this with [Vendor] if possible — it would help us to know whether this is on your roadmap and, if so, the expected timeframe. That would let us make an informed decision about how to proceed.

What this message does: it's honest, it creates a concrete deadline without being hostile, and it asks for information rather than making a demand. It's also very easy to escalate internally at the vendor's side, because it reads as a business signal, not a support complaint.

What it doesn't do: burn bridges. You may need to work with this vendor again. You may not actually migrate. Leave the door open.

## When to Build Around Them Instead

Escalation takes time and doesn't always work. At some point you have to make a call: keep waiting or build around it.

Build around it when:

- The workaround has a fixed cost and a clean implementation
- The vendor's response time is longer than your build time
- The problem is in a part of their product that's chronically under-invested
- Your users are paying the price of the wait

Keep escalating when:

- The fix requires access or data only the vendor has
- The workaround is fragile or carries ongoing maintenance cost
- The issue is at the boundary of their product — the kind of thing they'll fix once the pain is loud enough

Building around it isn't giving up. It's a business decision. You can do both: ship the workaround, keep the ticket open, and document internally what you built and why. If the vendor eventually ships the fix, you revisit. If they don't, you have a working system.

## The One Habit That Changes Everything

Every escalation message should be written as if the vendor's PM will forward it directly to their VP of Product with no additional context. Because sometimes that's exactly what happens.

That means no venting, no ticket history recaps, no "as I mentioned in my previous three messages." State the problem, state the impact, state what you need and by when. Make it easy to act on.

The vendors who move fastest on escalations don't do it because they're better at support. They do it because someone at your company made it trivially easy for the right person to understand why this mattered.
