A friend asks you to "have a look" at their website. You know the site — you've seen it in passing. It looks fine. Nice photos, warm copy, clearly someone who cares about their work. You say yes, assuming it'll take an hour.

Then you actually look, and you find that every single page tab reads "Home | Laravel." Not the business name, not a keyword — the name of the PHP framework it was built with. The default title configuration, never changed. And while you're at it, the sitemap returns a 404, there's no privacy policy anywhere, and the contact page actively warns visitors that emails might end up in their spam folder. The site has been live for years.

This is the situation I found myself in with a friend's photography website. Instead of combing through it manually, I decided to run it through an AI agent and see what came back. Here's what that process looked like, what the agent got right, and where it needed me to fill in the gaps.

## The Scope

The temptation with AI audits is to ask a vague question and get a wall of generic advice in return. "Audit this website" produces something between a listicle and a terms-of-service document — technically not wrong, practically useless.

The framing that actually worked was treating the audit like a staff engineer briefing: give the agent a scope, a severity model, and an output format.

```
Audit this website across six categories:
1. SEO (titles, meta, sitemap, structured data, Open Graph)
2. Technology (stack, security headers, performance signals, deliverability)
3. Content (copy clarity, completeness, consistency)
4. Conversion (CTAs, funnel, contact flow)
5. Design & UX (navigation, mobile, accessibility signals)
6. Legal (GDPR compliance for a German business)

For each finding, assign a severity: critical / warning / opportunity.
Output a findings table, category scores out of 100, and a prioritised action plan.
```

That last line matters. If you don't specify an output format, you get prose. Prose is fine to read; a structured findings table is actually usable.

## What the Agent Found

The results were more damning than I expected. Nine critical issues, nineteen warnings, eight opportunities. The category scores made the pattern clear:

| Category   | Score | Rating    |
| ---------- | ----- | --------- |
| SEO        | 28    | Very Poor |
| Technology | 55    | Fair      |
| Content    | 45    | Poor      |
| Conversion | 30    | Very Poor |
| Design     | 50    | Fair      |
| Marketing  | 38    | Poor      |

The "Laravel" title thing was the most visible, but not the most urgent. That honour went to the missing Datenschutzerklärung — the GDPR privacy policy required by German law for any website that processes personal data. Running a contact form without one exposes you to Abmahnung, which is Germany's peculiar mechanism for lawyers to send cease-and-desist letters on behalf of competitors or consumer protection organisations. It is not a theoretical risk. **This must be fixed before anything else.**

Second on the list: the contact page warning visitors to check their spam folder. This is a polite way of saying the site has no SPF, DKIM, or DMARC records configured — so mail servers receiving contact form submissions treat them as suspect. Every lead that comes in through that form is at risk. The agent caught this by reading the page copy. It didn't have access to DNS records, but the symptom was sitting right there in plain text, visible to anyone who visited the page.

## The Quick Wins That Always Appear

Here's a pattern I've noticed across every site audit I've done, AI-assisted or otherwise: the same cluster of issues always shows up on small business websites, and they're always fixable in under a day.

Missing page titles and meta descriptions are universal. Not because developers don't know about them — every Laravel developer knows `<title>` exists — but because they're invisible to the person who built the site. The site works; the titles are "fine." Nobody checks how the pages look in a Google search result or in a browser tab until someone external points it out.

The sitemap is the same story. `sitemap.xml` returning 404 means Google has no systematic way to discover and index the pages. The site isn't penalised — it just isn't helped. Generating and submitting a sitemap through Google Search Console takes about fifteen minutes and has an outsized impact on crawl coverage.

Open Graph tags are the other recurring gap. When you share a page on WhatsApp, Slack, or social media, the preview image and title are pulled from `og:image` and `og:title`. Without them, the platform picks something at random from the page — often a logo, a header image, or nothing. For a photography business where every shared link is an implicit portfolio sample, this matters.

None of these are clever or interesting problems. They're checkbox items. The value of an AI audit is that it checks all the boxes in ten minutes without needing anyone to remember which boxes exist.

## Where the Agent Needed Me

The audit report was good enough to hand over directly, but there were three areas where the agent's findings needed human translation.

**Visual quality.** The agent could tell me there was no portfolio page — `/portfolio` and `/galerie` both returned 404. It could not tell me whether the photos embedded elsewhere on the site were good. That's a meaningful gap for a photography business where the work is the product. "No gallery page" is a technical finding; "is the work compelling enough to convert" is a judgment call that requires eyes.

**Competitive context.** The agent scored the site's SEO at 28/100 based on what was missing. What it couldn't do was benchmark that against other Hamburg wedding photographers — whether the competition is equally invisible, slightly better, or a decade ahead. Knowing that would change the prioritisation. If everyone in the niche is missing structured data, the urgency of adding it shifts relative to something nobody else is doing.

**The actual problem behind the finding.** The Impressum listed the address as "6221629 Neu Wulmstorf" — the correct postcode is "21629". The agent flagged it as a typo in a legal document, which is accurate. What it couldn't assess is whether that's a typo, a CMS formatting issue, or a template bug that might appear in other places. That's the kind of question that takes thirty seconds for a developer to answer and would have taken the agent several tool calls to partially investigate.

## The Output Format

The deliverable from this particular audit was a canvas — a live React component rendered alongside the chat — showing the findings table with severity colour-coding, category score cards, and a prioritised action plan broken into three tiers: fix this week, fix this month, and consider next quarter.

The canvas format works well for sharing context during a conversation. For actually handing something over, I exported to a markdown report with the same structure. That's what I sent to my friend: a document she could open, read linearly, and hand to a developer with a clear priority order.

The format isn't the clever part. The clever part is that the agent produced a structured artifact in a single pass rather than requiring me to collate findings from five different tabs across Google Search Console, a meta tag checker, a privacy policy scanner, and a DNS lookup tool.

## When to Use This

The situations where an AI site audit earns its time back quickly are fairly specific. Pre-launch checks — before you point a domain at a new build — are the obvious one. Client handoffs, where you need to establish a baseline before taking over a site. And the "I haven't looked at this in a year" situation, which is what happened here: a site that works, receives real traffic, and converts occasional leads, but has been running on its original configuration without anyone poking at the infrastructure.

It is not a substitute for PageSpeed Insights with a real connection, for a proper accessibility audit, or for competitive research. It's a first pass — thorough, consistent, and fast — that surfaces the things that are unambiguously wrong so you can spend your judgment on the things that are genuinely ambiguous.

The "Home | Laravel" page title had been there for years. Nobody noticed because nobody looked. That's what this is for.
